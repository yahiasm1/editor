# Shapes

Four ways a body has worked. Pick by the subject, never force one, and adapt it: these are patterns, not templates.

## The everyday mechanism

For a thing the viewer touches every day that does something that looks impossible (`touchscreen`,
`iphone-keyboard`).

1. The impossible feat, the thing named in the first three words.
2. The viewer's question, short: "So how is that possible?"
3. The answer given away by second 8 to 10: "it isn't really X, it's Y."
4. One lived scene: "Imagine you're texting a friend and you've just typed T, I, M."
5. The chain, about half the words: one physical event per sentence, in time order, each caused by the last. The real
   term named once, after it was shown.
6. "But...": the second layer or the case where it fails, which proves the mechanism. "That's also why..." pays it off
   in the viewer's life (rain, touchscreen gloves).
7. A real source when there is one (the engineer's book, the patent).
8. The close echoing sentence one, resolved, then the ask with a named person to send it to.

## News to mechanism

For a story people heard: a launch, a hack, a leak, a creator's project (`ps5-on-pc`, `pewdiepie-abliteration`).

The hook. The news said straight, with who and one number. Why you care ("And I wanted to understand how you create
one"). The method's name ("The method is called abliteration"). Then the explanation, which is most of the reel, on
one picture held all the way. The catch, with its number. Something the viewer can use or check. A last line that is a
fact, not a verdict. The ask. The news is the door, not the subject.

## The straight concept

For a concept with no news (how a network, a data center, a payment works). The concept is the hook, said plainly
("Your authenticator app doesn't need the internet."), sometimes a test the viewer can do right now. Then start from
the naive version and let each problem force the next piece, named only once it is needed. One concrete case carried
from start to finish. A plain-number worked example where the abstract part is. One honest caveat. Everything
compressed into one line, then a reframe.

## The objection chain

For how a huge game or app pulls off something hard, or a fight between two sides (anti-cheat against cheats). The
simple answer first, then the objection a smart viewer would raise, then the deeper answer with its real engineering
name. Three or four objections is a reel. Tie each answer to something the viewer felt ("that's the lag you feel").
In a fight, each side's move is the other side's objection.
